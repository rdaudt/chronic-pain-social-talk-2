Treatment · Access · Ketamine
The Shortage No One Is Talking About
Dec. 2026
Expiration of DEA/HHS telemedicine flexibilities for ketamine prescribing — after which rules revert unless Congress or regulators extend again
2025
Year a major manufacturer discontinued its 100 mg/mL 10 mL injectable vials — the formulation most infusion clinics depend on for IV ketamine therapy

For patients relying on intravenous ketamine to manage treatment-resistant chronic pain — CRPS, neuropathic pain, fibromyalgia that has exhausted every other option — 2026 has brought a quiet, largely unreported crisis. The abrupt discontinuation of a key injectable formulation cascaded through infusion clinics, forcing cancellations, rationing, and in some cases abrupt treatment interruptions for patients who had finally found something that worked.

Supply remains tightest for injectable vials. Compounded oral and sublingual formulations, along with FDA-approved Spravato (esketamine nasal spray), have stayed comparatively accessible — but these are not clinically equivalent for every patient or every condition. What controls treatment-resistant depression may not replicate the infusion protocol that finally broke a pain cycle years in the making.

The DEA and HHS extended telemedicine prescribing flexibilities through December 2026, preserving access to ketamine prescriptions without an in-person visit — a lifeline for rural patients. But the infusion shortage has exposed what advocates have long argued: access to emerging treatments is structured by geography, income, and a system that cannot keep pace with its own evidence base.

Policy Update · May 13, 2026
The Map
Is Changing
Forty states allow some form of medical cannabis for pain. Now the rules are shifting again — and faster than patients can track.

On May 13, 2026 — five days ago — Georgia Governor Brian Kemp signed SB 220, expanding the state's medical cannabis program to add intractable pain as a qualifying condition. The law removed the previous 5% THC potency cap, replacing it with a 12,000-milligram possession limit for registered patients, and approved inhalation — including vaping — as a legal delivery method for adults 21 and over.

Georgia joins a wave of state-level movement. In Idaho, a ballot initiative would establish the state's first medical cannabis program, with chronic pain as a qualifying condition. At the federal level, an executive order initiated the formal reclassification of state-regulated medical marijuana and FDA-approved cannabis products to Schedule III — a historic shift with sweeping implications for banking access, insurance reimbursement, and the ability of researchers to finally study cannabis pain therapies at scale without DEA obstruction.

For chronic pain patients, the patchwork remains the central injustice: one state away from legal access, another from criminalization — and everywhere, an insurance system that still won't cover it regardless of what the state allows.

A 2026 JAMA Network Open systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed that spinal cord stimulation outperforms conventional medical management for chronic back and leg pain on both pain relief and functional improvement — the most comprehensive evidence synthesis to date. Yet for many patients, the barrier is not evidence but coverage: prior authorization requirements, step therapy mandates, and arbitrary denials that force patients through failed treatments before insurers will consider a proven one.

A case presented at the 2026 Association of Academic Physiatrists National Meeting added an intriguing new variable: a patient with post-laminectomy syndrome maintained substantial pain relief and functional improvement nearly one year after a temporary SCS trial — without proceeding to permanent implantation. Researchers are now investigating whether the trial period itself may durably rewire maladaptive pain circuits in some patients, opening a potentially lower-risk and lower-cost pathway to relief.

System Failure · Emergency Care
The ER
Is Not
For You
"Doctors aren't really equipped to use their standard diagnostic and treatment toolkit for chronic pain."

When acute flares strike — a herniated disc that shifts overnight, a CRPS crisis, a migraine that won't break — chronic pain patients are often left with no option but the emergency room. What they frequently find is not care, but suspicion.

The label "drug seeker" follows patients from triage to discharge. Staff who encounter the same face repeatedly see a problem patient rather than a person in unmanaged crisis. A 2026 podcast episode — The ER Nightmare For Chronic Pain Patients (Apple Podcasts) — catalogued what advocates have documented for years: patients dismissed mid-flare, denied adequate treatment, sent home without referrals.

A survey by the American Chronic Pain Association and the American College of Emergency Medicine Physicians identified the main drivers: severe pain, fear that something new is wrong, and lack of insurance or access to a specialist. These are not drug-seeking behaviors. They are the predictable consequences of a care gap that the rest of the system refuses to fill.

Patients have also reported developing PTSD specifically from ER encounters — the cumulative weight of disbelief and undertreated pain. "In the emergency room, patients have been disregarded, their pain minimized," the U.S. Pain Foundation notes. "It takes pushing and pushing to get someone to actually listen." For people already exhausted from years of not being believed, that bar is impossible.

Identity · Personal Narrative
The Other
Patient

Behind every person living with chronic pain, there is often someone else quietly absorbing the weight: a spouse managing medication schedules at 3 a.m., a parent who reorganizes the household around flare calendars, a partner who has become — without any ceremony or support — a full-time caregiver. Their suffering rarely appears in clinical notes.

The U.S. Pain Foundation hosts a free monthly support group specifically for caregivers and care partners of people with chronic pain — recognizing that caregiver burnout is not personal failure but a predictable outcome of a system with no plan for the people holding everything together.

More than 60% of caregivers experience burnout symptoms. Between 20 and 30 percent are in active burnout at any given time. The CDC frames family caregiving as a chronic stressor in its own right — one that elevates risk of stress-related illness, mental health decline, and delayed personal healthcare. "Adding a loved one's chronic pain to the relationship magnifies the difficulty and intensifies the stress." Doing so indefinitely, without respite, produces compassion fatigue and eventually collapse.

Respite care — temporary relief provided by a third party — is among the most evidence-supported interventions for preventing caregiver burnout. So are peer support groups. Both remain scarce, unevenly distributed, and almost never covered by insurance.

Chronic pain is not an adult condition. Between 11% and 38% of children and adolescents cope with it daily — a range reflecting not clinical ambiguity but profound variation in how pediatric pain is measured and reported across studies. A 2026 systematic review of qualitative evidence (PMC 11864317) found that pain impacted relationships with family and friends, altered educational trajectories, and changed young people's "sense of self-identity" — before they were old enough to vote or navigate an insurance claim.

The pandemic compounded an already difficult picture. A 2026 study in Frontiers in Pain Research found that COVID-19 caused major discontinuities in healthcare for youth with chronic pain — disruptions that increased pain severity and co-occurring anxiety and depression, particularly among the most vulnerable. The intersection of pain and mental health in young people is not incidental; it is structural.

The long-term consequences are documented in PAIN (PMC 7001863): adolescent-onset chronic pain reduces the odds of completing high school by 34% and college by 17%. Children miss roughly one in five school days. When they age out of pediatric care at 18, many fall into gaps that take years to close. This is not a niche problem. It is a pipeline to adult disadvantage that the system has largely chosen not to look at.

Science · Biomarkers · Neuroimmunology
Blood
Doesn't
Lie

For decades, chronic pain was dismissed partly because it left no visible trace in a blood panel. That is ending. A 2025 study in Nature Human Behaviour demonstrated that risk scores derived from 52 blood markers — spanning inflammatory, immune, metabolic, and hematological assays — accurately predict both specific pain conditions and patients' self-reported pain severity.

The strongest single predictor: C-reactive protein (CRP), which partially mediates the link between composite pain scores and depression risk — offering a biological explanation for a connection patients have always known but been told was "just stress."

  • CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α elevated across chronic non-cancer pain populations
  • TSPO — a neuroinflammation marker — upregulated in discrete brain regions after peripheral nerve injury
  • Low-grade inflammation predicts measurably reduced response to behavioral pain treatment

"Converging evidence reframes chronic pain as a disorder maintained by maladaptive neuroimmune interactions across the peripheral and central nervous systems." — Frontiers in Immunology, 2026

A 2026 review in Frontiers in Immunology synthesized what neuroimmunology is now revealing: chronic pain is not only a nervous system problem — it is an immune system problem, and potentially a treatable one if the right targets can be identified and reached.

One finding carries particularly urgent clinical significance: patients with elevated low-grade inflammation show measurably less improvement from behavioral pain treatments — CBT, ACT, multidisciplinary programs. This does not invalidate those treatments. It suggests that for a subset of patients, anti-inflammatory strategies may be a prerequisite, not an alternative. The biopsychosocial model has always been right about pain's multidimensionality; biomarkers are beginning to give it molecular precision.

The implication for patients who have been told "you're not responding to treatment" is profound: the problem may not be their effort or their attitude. It may be measurable in their blood — and one day, treatable at that level.

System · Opioid Policy · Patient Rights
Sign Here,
Trust Us
Opioid treatment agreements are now required in most US jurisdictions. Evidence that they reduce harm remains elusive. Evidence that they harm some patients is growing.
What They Require

Opioid treatment agreements — also called pain contracts, narcotic agreements, or controlled-substance agreements — are documents patients must sign to receive opioid medication. Standard requirements include arriving on time to appointments, being respectful to staff, submitting to random urine drug tests and pill counts, and acknowledging that prescribing may stop if any term is violated.

Non-compliance may mean discharge from a practice — sometimes without referrals, sometimes mid-treatment, sometimes after years of stable care. The agreement is presented as mutual. In practice, its terms are set entirely by one party.

What the Evidence Shows
"Some agreements are stigmatizing or coercive and jeopardize trust, with content varying widely and efficacy uncertain."

A 2024 review in The Milbank Quarterly (PMC 11576586) found that OTAs are now required in most US jurisdictions — yet well-established evidence that they reduce opioid misuse, diversion, or overdose does not exist. The CDC and professional societies recommend them. Courts are treating them as legally binding documents, increasingly influencing the outcomes of civil and criminal cases involving pain physicians and their patients.

What Patients Experience

For many people with chronic pain, signing an OTA is experienced as an accusation delivered before any transgression has occurred. The documents presuppose the likelihood of misuse — requiring ongoing proof of legitimacy from people already exhausted by years of not being believed.

Advocates have long argued for better-designed agreements focused on shared decision-making, disclosure, and informed consent rather than behavioral surveillance. The Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine published a reform framework in 2016. A decade later, it remains widely unimplemented — while the coercive version continues to spread.
